Where the Funnel Breaks

2,515,155 real applicants (107 internal test accounts excluded throughout — see the note at the bottom) tried to get verified. 7.93% never made it — well above the 5% line the business treats as acceptable. This is a plain-English walk through why, built entirely from the data, with the worst single cause isolated and sized.

First, the vocabulary

Four checks, one waterfall

Every applicant falls through up to four layers of checks, cheapest and fastest first. The business calls these L1–L4. Knowing what each one does makes the rest of this page click.

L1 · Idology

The front door

Instant, automatic. Checks name, date of birth, address and SSN against public records in seconds. Most people clear it and are done.

84.8% of everyone stops here
L2 · LexisNexis

SSN backup

Automatic re-check, mainly for a mismatched or missing Social Security Number. Two separate integrations feed this one field (more on that below).

Also covers Persona—SSN mode
L3 · ACRO / Persona

Identity backup

For name, address or DOB problems. Persona’s IDV mode asks for a photo ID + selfie — slower, more friction, but resolves what automation can’t.

Highest-friction automated check
L4 · Manual review

The human

A trained analyst looks at the case personally. The safety net — meant to catch anyone the automated checks couldn’t clear.

Clears ~32% of what reaches it
The funnel

Most people never even reach this chart

88.5% of applicants pass Idology outright, or are cleared through another door entirely, and they’re done in seconds. This chart is only about the 288,028 people (11.5%) who failed that very first check — because that’s where the real funnel, and the real problem, live.

Read it left to right. Width = number of people. Blue = given a fair second chance. Red = never was. Hover any bar or block for its exact count and the query it comes from.

Of the 288,028 people who failed the first check, 101,162 — better than a third — were never given a second one at all. Only 3 of them ever got verified anyway.

Ruling out a bad month

This has been happening for 41 straight months

If the rate had spiked recently, the fix might be a rollback. It never spikes — it sits within half a point of ~7.9% from January 2023 straight through May 2026. This is how the system behaves by design, not an incident.

Two more cracks

Worth flagging, even though they’re smaller

3.7%

An undocumented second front door

94,183 people (3.7%) never see Idology at all — they enter through a LexisNexis integration that clears 99% of everyone it sees. Either they were screened elsewhere first, or this door needs a lock. The data can’t tell which — a question for engineering, not a routing fix.

69.8%

The safety net has a hole

213,048 people failed every automated check that ran on them. Only 30.2% ever reached a human reviewer. Manual review overall clears about a third of what reaches it (~32%) — coverage, not quality, is the gap here.

0.87%

The best tool is barely used

Persona’s photo-ID-plus-selfie check clears 86.3% of people it’s tried on, versus 30.7% for people who never got it — yet it runs on under 1% of first-check failures.

Design vs. reality

What the brief says vs. what the data shows

Confirming the documented waterfall against the data was explicitly part of this task. Five places it diverges:

L1
The design says

Idology is the single primary check everyone goes through first.

The data shows

94,183 people (3.7%) skip it entirely and enter through LexisNexis instead — a second, undocumented front door.

L1
The design says

Passing Idology means verified — the person exits the waterfall immediately.

The data shows

130,713 people passed Idology and had LexisNexis run anyway; 57,822 of them failed it and were still verified. The check doesn’t gate the outcome.

L2
The design says

A failed check is logged as SSN vs. non-SSN and routed accordingly.

The data shows

35.1% of Idology failures — 101,162 people — are routed nowhere at all. This is the main defect on this page.

L2
The design says

On the SSN path, “older accounts” get routed to legacy Persona-SSN instead of LexisNexis.

The data shows

No age or account-vintage signal exists in who gets Persona-SSN. The rule isn’t reproducible from this data.

L4
The design says

Manual review is the safety net — it catches everyone the automated checks couldn’t clear.

The data shows

Works well when reached (32% cleared) — but 69.8% of eligible people never reach it.

Reading this responsibly

Assumptions & data caveats

Every judgment call behind the numbers above, stated plainly — so any of them can be defended, or revised, under questioning.

1

Denominators, and why 107 test accounts are excluded everywhere on this page

Rows are never deleted in this analysis — every exclusion is a flag, so the reader picks the denominator instead of the loader picking it for them. Every number on this page excludes the 107 internal Test_user accounts (flagged via is_test_user, filtered with where not is_test_user in every query). The 7.93% headline (199,421 of 2,515,155) still includes 843 real users who never had any check run at all — that is a real, honestly-counted non-verification, not a data artefact (see caveat 2). Every number on this page has been re-verified against a live query with test accounts consistently excluded.

2

The trap in this dataset

One field, kyc_source, records which check actually cleared a person. It’s empty for 7.84% of users, and it’s tempting to read “empty” as bad data and drop those rows. Don’t — it’s empty because nobody cleared them. Every one of those ~197K rows has zero PASS results anywhere. Dropping them doesn’t clean the data; it deletes 99% of the rejections and reports a fake 0.09% instead of the real 7.93%.

3

“LexisNexis” is really two different checks

Two separate integrations (ProviderA_Lexis_Nexis, ProviderB_Lexis_Nexis) write to the same result column. ProviderA sits in primary position (93,319 of its 149,364 users never saw Idology); ProviderB sits in secondary position (11,677 of its 15,998 arrive after an Idology failure). Averaging them together blurs a front door with a backup check.

4

Reviewer comments cover ~3% of users

Free-text notes exist for about 74,400 of 2,515,155 users and skew heavily toward manual review. They’re useful for understanding why reviewed cases failed, but say nothing about the 101,162 stranded users — who have zero comments, because nothing ever looked at them.

5

The 48,649-person recovery figure is a projection

It comes from a like-for-like comparison group (users who failed the same check and were routed onward), not a controlled experiment. Stranding is uniform across every partner and year, which supports the comparison — but the honest way to confirm it is to actually route a sample of stranded users and measure.

6

A naive line count overstates the dataset by 29 rows

wc -l on the source CSV reports 2,515,291 lines; the true record count is 2,515,262 (of which 107 are internal test accounts, excluded from every figure above). Eight reviewer_comment values contain embedded newlines inside quoted fields. Every number on this page comes from a real CSV parser (FORMAT csv), not a line count.

7

Age isn’t used here, and couldn’t be precisely anyway

age_at_enrollment is derived from a month-only date of birth (MM/YYYY) and is accurate to roughly ±1 year — casting it to a full date would invent a day of precision the data doesn’t have. Not used as a driver on this page (see the “older accounts” finding above, which found no such signal), but worth flagging since a ~104-year-old tail exists in the raw data.

8

State isn’t used as a driver here either, and the raw field can’t be trusted if it ever is

The raw state field mixes codes and full names — OH and Ohio are 2 of 103 distinct values for 50-odd states. A naive GROUP BY state would understate every affected state and invent duplicate rows. Any future cut by geography must use the normalised state_code column.
